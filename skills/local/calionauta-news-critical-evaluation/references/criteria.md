## Organizing principle

The five dimensions split the communication situation into parts that cannot be the same thing:

| Dimension | Object it examines | Core question |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Source | The speaker and the route the message took | Who is behind this and what do they want? |
| 2. Claim and evidence | The proposition and its support | What is asserted and how well is it supported? |
| 3. Framing | The telling of it (form, selection, rhetoric) | How is it told and who does that telling serve? |
| 4. Context | The world around the story | Where does it sit relative to knowledge, time and rival accounts? |
| 5. Reader | The person evaluating | What is my own bias and how much does this matter to me? |

Within each dimension the criteria differ by the specific question asked. If a finding seems to fit two criteria, use the tie-break rules at the end.

## Dimension 1 - Source

### 1.1 Chain of custody
**Question:** Who produced this, who relayed it, by what route, and is the artifact itself genuine?
**Check:** original producer vs. publisher vs. amplifier (a researcher, a PR team, a journalist, an influencer); whether the quote, image, video, document or screenshot is authentic and unaltered; whether it is an impersonation of a real outlet.
**Not this:** whether the producer has an agenda (1.2); whether the claim has evidence behind it (2.2).

### 1.2 Interests of the source
**Question:** Who pays, who profits, and why is this out now?
**Check:** ownership and funding of the outlet or study; business model (clicks, subscriptions, ads, sponsored content); conflicts of interest of named sources; timing against launches, fundraising, elections, regulatory decisions, earnings.
**Not this:** how the text is slanted (3.1, 3.2). Interests explain *why*; dimension 3 records *what* is on the page.

### 1.3 Track record and accountability
**Question:** Does this outlet or author verify, correct and disclose?
**Check:** corrections history; editorial standards and separation of news from opinion; named authorship; transparency about methods and sources; past errors in similar stories.
**Not this:** quality of this specific story's evidence (2.2).

## Dimension 2 - Claim and evidence

### 2.1 Claim clarity
**Question:** What exactly is being claimed, and is it fact, inference or opinion?
**Check:** restate the central claim in one sentence; label each supporting statement as observed, inferred or opinion; headline and visuals match what the body supports (a mismatch is a red flag).
**Not this:** whether the claim is true (2.2, 2.3).

### 2.2 Evidence quality and traceability
**Question:** What supports the claim, and can it be traced to something primary?
**Check:** primary source located (study, filing, dataset, document, original footage); direct vs. second-hand evidence; named vs. anonymous sources; independent corroboration (sources that do not trace back to each other); sample size and method for studies; original context preserved (not clipped or relabeled).
**Not this:** whether the reasoning from the evidence to the conclusion holds (2.3).

### 2.3 Reasoning validity
**Question:** Given the evidence, does the conclusion follow?
**Check:** implicit premises that must be true; numbers given with a baseline and a denominator ("doubled" from what?); correlation presented as causation; alternative explanations ignored; extrapolation beyond the data.
**Not this:** how good the evidence is (2.2).

## Dimension 3 - Framing

### 3.1 Selection and emphasis
**Question:** Which facts and angle are foregrounded, and which are left out?
**Check:** the dominant frame (threat, progress, scandal, inevitability, conflict); what the headline and first paragraph select; relevant facts that are missing; what changes if the frame is inverted.
**Not this:** whose voices appear (3.3); word choice and emotion (3.2).

### 3.2 Language and persuasive intent
**Question:** What emotion and action is the text built to trigger?
**Check:** loaded adjectives and verbs; certainty beyond what the evidence supports; urgency cues; calls to share, buy, fear, join, wait; whether the action invited benefits whoever produced the piece.
**Not this:** which facts were chosen (3.1); who gains from the story prevailing (3.3).

### 3.3 Stakes and voices
**Question:** If this telling prevails, who gains, who loses, and who is missing from the story?
**Check:** who bears the costs and who collects the benefits (often different groups); who speaks as a subject versus who is only described as "the affected"; stakeholders with direct experience who are not quoted. Compare against the perspective sample from research: which perspectives cover the story and which are silent.
**Not this:** the source's own conflicts of interest (1.2); the content of rival accounts (4.2). Here you record *absence* and *distribution*.

## Dimension 4 - Context

### 4.1 Maturity of knowledge
**Question:** Is this settled, actively debated, or too recent to understand?
**Check:** consensus among relevant experts; time since the event; whether early reports have already been corrected; what is not yet known and when it will be.
**Not this:** strategic timing of publication (1.2).

### 4.2 Competing accounts
**Question:** What are the strongest rival interpretations, who holds them, and what is their evidence?
**Check:** the *best* version of the opposing view (what its most capable defenders say, not the caricature in the article), drawn from outlets in the perspective sample rather than from how the article describes its opponents; the authority behind it (direct experience, own data, a different position in the chain); the same tests (1.x, 2.x) applied to it; what it reveals that the dominant story hides; whether the disagreement is factual or about values.
**Not this:** whether the article included these voices (3.3).

### 4.3 Precedent and novelty
**Question:** Has something like this happened before, and is this new or repackaged?
**Check:** historical analogues and what happened; base rates for claims of this type (breakthroughs, scandals, "first ever"); whether an old story is being re-presented as new.
**Not this:** whether the current facts are settled (4.1).

---

## Dimension 5 - Reader

### 5.1 My own bias
**Question:** Do I want this to be true, and am I applying the same standard either way?
**Check:** emotional reaction before reading (stop before sharing); whether a story that annoys me would get the same scrutiny as one that pleases me. In this skill: state the model's own possible blind spots (e.g., source mix skewed to one region, language or political leaning, or a perspective sample that came back thin) and flag them.
**Not this:** the text's emotional design (3.2).

### 5.2 Stakes and decision relevance
**Question:** Does this change a decision, and how much checking is it worth?
**Check:** what the user would do differently if the story were true or false; cost of being wrong; recommended depth of further verification.
**Not this:** how important the story is to the world.

## Tie-break rules

1. Something about the **source's interests** -> 1.2, even if it explains slant. Something **visible in the text** -> dimension 3, even if interests caused it.
2. A perspective **absent** from the article -> 3.3. What that perspective **says and how strong it is** -> 4.2.
3. **Quality of evidence** -> 2.2. **Whether the conclusion follows** -> 2.3.
4. **Why now** (strategic timing) -> 1.2. **How settled the knowledge is** -> 4.1.
5. **Authenticity of an artifact** -> 1.1. **Whether the artifact proves the claim** -> 2.2.
6. **Emotion the text is designed to trigger** -> 3.2. **Emotion the reader brings** -> 5.1.

## Quick mode: 6 pocket questions

Run these when the user asks for a quick read. Output the Bottom line, the one-sentence claim, who gains/loses, the strongest counter-story, and the six answers (no 13-row table).

1. Who produced and amplified this, and what do independent sources say about them? (1.1, 1.3)
2. Who gains and who loses if this is the story that prevails? (1.2, 3.3)
3. What is the primary evidence, and is it direct and independent? (2.2)
4. What must be true for the conclusion to hold? (2.3)
5. What is the strongest counter-story, and who is missing? (3.3, 4.2)
6. Is this settled or too early to tell? (4.1)

## Where these come from

The structure draws on established media-literacy work: lateral reading and SIFT (Caulfield), the Center for Media Literacy's five key questions, NAMLE's "who benefits / who is harmed", Stony Brook's verification, independence and accountability, and Wardle's categories of information disorder. The strongest-counter-story test (4.2) and the strategic-timing check (1.2) are additions that those frameworks cover only partly.
